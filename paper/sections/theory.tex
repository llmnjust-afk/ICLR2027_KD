\section{Theoretical Analysis}
\label{sec:theory}

We develop a formal framework explaining why per-class adaptive guidance outperforms fixed guidance, why cluster entropy and intra-class variance are the effective complexity factors, and why inter-class separability is not. Our analysis proceeds under a Gaussian mixture model for the class-conditional distribution~\cite{ho2022classifierfree, peebles2022scalable}.

\paragraph{Setup.}
Consider a class $c$ whose conditional distribution is a $K_c$-component Gaussian mixture:
\begin{equation}
p(\mathbf{x} \mid c) = \sum_{k=1}^{K_c} \pi_{c,k}\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x};\,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c,k},\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{c,k}),
\label{eq:gmm}
\end{equation}
where $\pi_{c,k} > 0$ are mode proportions satisfying $\sum_k \pi_{c,k} = 1$. The cluster entropy $H_c = -\sum_k \pi_{c,k}\log\pi_{c,k}$ measures the balance of mode sizes, and the intra-class variance $v_c = \mathrm{tr}[\bar{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}_c]$ measures the total feature spread. Under CFG with scale $\lambda$, the effective conditional distribution is $p_\lambda(\mathbf{x}\mid c) \propto p(\mathbf{x}\mid c)^{1+\lambda}\,p(\mathbf{x})^{-\lambda}$.

\begin{lemma}[CFG Mode Sharpening and Reweighting]
\label{lem:cfg-modes}
For well-separated modes, the CFG-modified distribution is approximately $p_\lambda(\mathbf{x}\mid c) \approx \sum_{k} \tilde{\pi}_{c,k}(\lambda)\,\mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{x};\,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c,k},\,\tfrac{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{c,k}}{1+\lambda}\right)$, where $\tilde{\pi}_{c,k}(\lambda) = \pi_{c,k}^{1+\lambda}\big/\sum_j \pi_{c,j}^{1+\lambda}$. Thus CFG has two effects: (i) \emph{sharpening}---each mode's covariance is scaled by $1/(1+\lambda)$---and (ii) \emph{reweighting}---dominant modes receive proportionally more weight.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proposition}[Diversity Cost of Guidance]
\label{prop:div-cost}
The KL divergence $D_c(\lambda) := \mathrm{KL}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\pi}_c \,\|\, \tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_c(\lambda)\right)$ satisfies $D_c(\lambda) = \lambda\,H_c + \log Z_c(\lambda)$, with $D_c''(0) = \mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}_c}[\log\pi_{c,k}]$, which is \emph{decreasing in} $H_c$: balanced (high-entropy) classes incur lower diversity cost.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proposition}[Optimal Per-Class Guidance]
\label{prop:opt-lambda}
Define the per-class generation utility $U_c(\lambda) = (1+\lambda)\,v_c - \beta\,D_c(\lambda)$, where the first term captures the \emph{discriminative benefit} of guidance---sharper modes (covariance scaled by $1/(1+\lambda)$ per Lemma~\ref{lem:cfg-modes}) increase the effective feature separation by a factor of $(1+\lambda)$, measured by the trace of the sharpened covariance $v_c$---and the second term captures the \emph{diversity cost} from mode reweighting (Proposition~\ref{prop:div-cost}). The parameter $\beta > 0$ controls the trade-off between discriminative sharpness and distributional fidelity. The optimal guidance $\lambda_c^* = \arg\max_\lambda U_c(\lambda)$ satisfies, to second order:
\begin{equation}
\lambda_c^* \approx \frac{v_c}{\beta\,\sigma_c^2},
\label{eq:opt-lambda}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_c^2 = \mathrm{Var}_{\boldsymbol{\pi}_c}[\log\pi_{c,k}]$. Consequently, $\lambda_c^*$ is \emph{increasing in} $v_c$ (intra-class variance) and \emph{increasing in} $H_c$ (cluster entropy, since $\sigma_c^2$ decreases with $H_c$). The linear-in-$\lambda$ benefit term is valid for small $\lambda$ (the regime used in practice, $\lambda \in [0, 0.06]$); for large $\lambda$, higher-order terms in $D_c(\lambda)$ dominate and the utility eventually decreases, consistent with the empirical finding that excessive guidance hurts performance (Fixed $\lambda = 0.10$ underperforms unguided, \Cref{tab:sota-main}).
\end{proposition}

\begin{theorem}[Adaptivity Gap]
\label{thm:adaptivity-gap}
Let $\bar{\lambda} = \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^C \lambda_c^*$ be the average optimal guidance (the best fixed guidance) and $\{\lambda_c^*\}_{c=1}^C$ the per-class optimal. The \emph{adaptivity gap}---the average utility gain from per-class adaptation---is $\Delta = \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^C \frac{\beta\,\sigma_c^2}{2}\,(\lambda_c^* - \bar{\lambda})^2 \geq 0$, which equals zero iff all classes have identical optimal guidance and is increasing in the cross-class variance of $\lambda_c^*$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{lemma}[Separability Invariance]
\label{lem:sep-invariance}
Under the well-separated modes assumption, the optimal per-class guidance $\lambda_c^*$ depends only on intra-class properties ($v_c$, $H_c$) and is independent of inter-class separability $d_c$.
\end{lemma}

\paragraph{Connection to empirical findings.}
These results explain five key observations: \emph{(1)} cluster entropy is the most effective single factor because high-entropy classes have near-zero diversity cost and sustain stronger guidance (Proposition~\ref{prop:div-cost}); \emph{(2)} separability is the least effective factor because the optimal $\lambda_c^*$ is independent of it (Lemma~\ref{lem:sep-invariance}); \emph{(3)} the two-factor design $(H_c, v_c)$ is optimal since $\lambda_c^*$ is increasing in both (Proposition~\ref{prop:opt-lambda}); \emph{(4)} CAGS gains scale with class count because the adaptivity gap grows with cross-class heterogeneity (Theorem~\ref{thm:adaptivity-gap}); and \emph{(5)} CAGS is neutral on 10-class subsets where complexity variation is narrow ($\Delta \approx 0$). We empirically validate the adaptivity gap prediction by measuring mode structure heterogeneity across datasets (Appendix~\ref{sec:theory-proofs}): the fraction of classes with multi-mode structure ($K > 2$) increases from $0\%$ at $C{=}10$ to $60\%$ at $C{=}1000$, tracking the actual CAGS gain. The connection between the linear theoretical prediction $\lambda_c^* \propto v_c/\sigma_c^2$ and the sigmoid-based implementation is discussed in Appendix~\ref{sec:theory-proofs}.

\paragraph{Why TAGS and IAST fail.}
\tags{} varies $\lambda$ across timesteps but applies the same $\lambda(t)$ to all classes at each $t$. Since $\lambda_c^*$ depends on intra-class properties, not the noise level, the optimal $\lambda(t)$ at each timestep is the cross-class average $\bar{\lambda}$, yielding an adaptivity gap exactly $T$ times the fixed-guidance gap---no improvement. \iast{} compresses the guidance range by a factor $s(\text{IPC})$, reducing the adaptivity gap to $s^2 \cdot \Delta_{\text{CAGS}}$; at IPC=1, $s \approx 0.29$, eliminating $92\%$ of the per-class differentiation. Both modules vary $\lambda$ along dimensions (time, IPC) where the optimal $\lambda_c^*$ is constant, failing to exploit the class axis where $\lambda_c^*$ actually differs. Proofs and detailed derivations are in Appendix~\ref{sec:theory-proofs}.
